## 1. Understanding What a Futures Trade Actually Does

Let's say you want to trade BTC.

In Spot trading, you use USDT to buy BTC.

In Futures, you are: opening a directional position on BTCUSDT.

There are generally only two directions: **Long** and **Short**.

| Direction | Meaning |
|-----------|---------|
| **Long** | You believe the price will go up |
| **Short** | You believe the price will go down |

So the core of futures trading is NOT "buying coins." It is:

1. Choose a direction
2. Decide position size
3. Set risk parameters
4. Wait for price movement
5. Close the position

## 3. Why Does Futures Show "No Funds" When Spot Has USDT?

This is one of the most common issues beginners face. The reason is usually: your USDT is in your Spot Wallet, but Futures uses a separate funding area.

You need to do an **internal transfer**:
Spot Wallet → Transfer → USDT-M Futures Wallet → Enter USDT Amount → Confirm
This step is NOT a withdrawal. It's NOT an on-chain transfer either. It's simply a reallocation of funds within your Binance account.

## 5. Make Four Decisions Before Opening a Position

Don't just click Long or Short the moment you see price movement. Before starting a trade, decide on at least four things:

| # | Decision |
|---|----------|
| ① | Direction: Long or Short |
| ② | Position size: How much capital to use |
| ③ | Leverage: Low or high |
| ④ | Where to exit if wrong: That's your Stop Loss |

If you haven't thought these four things through, jumping in directly can easily turn into emotional trading.

## 6. Decide Direction First, Then Decide Whether to Place an Order

The logic of Long and Short is simple. But direction shouldn't be a random guess based on a single candlestick. Beginners should ask themselves:

- Why am I entering this trade?
- If price moves against me, where will I exit?
- What is the maximum loss I can accept on this trade?

Answer these first, then decide whether to open a position.

---

## 7. Position Size Matters More Than Leverage Multiplier

Many beginners focus first on: 5x, 10x, 20x, 50x. But what truly affects risk isn't just leverage. It also includes:

- Your principal
- Position size
- Margin mode
- Stop loss placement
- Market volatility

At the same 10x leverage, different position sizes produce completely different risk levels. So instead of only asking "What leverage should I use?", ask: "How big is this position actually? If stopped out, how much will I lose?"

---

## 8. Don't Choose Isolated vs. Cross Randomly

When opening a position, you'll typically see two margin modes:

| Mode | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Isolated** | Each position uses relatively independent margin. Easier to observe the risk of a single position |
| **Cross** | Available funds in your Futures Wallet may jointly support the position. If the market keeps moving against you, more funds could be affected |

For those just starting out, **Isolated** is generally easier to understand. At the very least, you can clearly see: how much margin this specific trade is using.

---

## 9. What Is Entry Price?

Entry Price is your average opening price. For example, if you open a Long on BTCUSDT near a certain price, that average fill price becomes your Entry Price. Whether the current price is above or below your Entry Price affects your floating PnL. After opening a position, the first thing to look at isn't "what percentage am I up," but: how far is the current price from my Entry Price?

---

## 10. Why Mark Price Matters

Many beginners only watch the latest fill price. But in Futures, Mark Price is also important. It's typically used to reduce the impact of short-term abnormal fill prices on liquidation calculations. On your position page, you'll see Last Price and Mark Price — they aren't always identical. When assessing position risk, don't just look at the last fill price; pay attention to Mark Price too.

---

## 11. Liquidation Price Is a Number You Cannot Ignore

Every time you open a position, you should immediately check the **Liquidation Price**. If the market keeps moving against you and the position risk reaches a certain threshold, the system may force-close your position.

In any futures trade, you should know at least three prices simultaneously:

- Entry Price
- Stop Loss
- Liquidation Price

The ideal scenario: your Stop Loss should help you control risk before you ever get close to liquidation. **Don't treat "waiting for liquidation" as your stop-loss method.**

---

## 12. Decide Stop Loss BEFORE Opening, Not After a Loss

Many people make this mistake: open first, then think about whether to stop loss after losing money. This easily turns into a much bigger loss.

The better sequence is: decide Stop Loss → decide position size → finally open the position. Because only once you know your stop-loss level can you work backward: how big should this trade be?

---

## 13. Don't Set Take Profit by Gut Feeling Either

Besides stop loss, you also need to think: if the market actually moves in your expected direction, where will you exit? That's your Take Profit.

A common beginner problem: holding losing positions too long, but closing winning positions too quickly. This makes it very hard to build a consistent trading logic over time.

So plan ahead: stop-loss level, first take-profit target, whether to close in batches, and how to handle the remaining position.

---

## 14. How to Use Market Orders and Limit Orders?

| Type | Description | Pros | Cons |
|------|-------------|------|------|
| **Market Order** | Enters at the best available market price | Fast execution | Fill price may differ slightly during volatile moves |
| **Limit Order** | You set the price you want to fill at | More price control | May not fill if price never reaches your level |

If this is your first time practicing Futures, start with a small position to get familiar with the difference between the two.

## 15. What to Watch After Opening a Position

Once a position is open, don't just stare at the red/green PnL. Check:

- Entry Price
- Mark Price
- Position Size
- Margin
- Unrealized PnL
- Liquidation Price
- Stop Loss
- Take Profit

Looking at all these fields together tells you what state your position is actually in.

---

## 16. Floating Profit ≠ Money Already Made

If your position shows +10 USDT, that's typically just unrealized PnL. As long as the position is still open and market prices keep moving, that number can still change. To truly end a trade, you need to close the position. Only after the position is closed does unrealized PnL convert to realized PnL.

---

## 17. How to Close a Position

When you decide to end a trade, you can choose to close. Common methods include: market close, limit close, close all, and partial close. Beginners MUST learn how to close a position before they start opening positions frequently. Because knowing how to enter but not how to exit is a very dangerous habit in Futures.

---

## 18. Check Trade History After Closing

After a trade ends, review: open time, close time, average fill price, realized PnL, trading fees, and Funding Fee. Don't just look at whether you made or lost money. More importantly, ask: did this trade execute according to the original plan?

---

## 19. What Is Funding Fee?

Perpetual futures commonly involve Funding Fees. This is NOT simply an opening or closing fee. Funding Fees are periodic payments exchanged between long and short positions based on market mechanisms. So if you hold a Futures position for an extended period, you can't just calculate entry and exit fees — you also need to factor in the Funding Rate.
